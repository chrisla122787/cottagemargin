Batch scaling

Scaling is linear: every quantity and cost multiplies by the same factor, so cost per item stays the same. Shown quantities are rounded for the kitchen — weights to whole grams, volumes and counts to the nearest quarter — but costs are always figured from the exact amounts.

    Careful: a margin under 20% is very thin for baked goods. One price rise at the store, one spoiled tray, or one slow market day can turn this recipe into a loss. Once your labor is counted, many selling bakers aim for 50% or more.
    That margin won't work: a margin of 100% or more can't be priced. Set your target margin below 100% — 40–60% is typical for baked goods.
    Those numbers can't work together: your target margin plus the payment-fee percentage adds up to 100% or more, so no price can deliver that margin after fees. Lower the target margin or the fee percentage.
    Unit mismatch: one or more ingredient lines use a unit that can't be converted for that ingredient (for example, grams for something bought by the count). Those lines are counted as $0 until the unit is fixed.
    Wholesale warning: at this wholesale price you make nothing — or lose money — on every item sold, once your cost per item sold (and payment fees, if set) are counted. Raise the wholesale price, lower the discount, or check the lowest wholesale price shown above.
